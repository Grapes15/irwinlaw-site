Dividing Property and Debt

Minnesota divides marital property equitably, which is not the same as equally. Most of the fight is over what counts as marital and what it is worth.

Marital and nonmarital

Property either spouse acquired during the marriage is presumed marital, whichever name is on the title (Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b). Property is nonmarital if one spouse owned it before the marriage, received it as a gift or inheritance made to that spouse alone, or can trace it to one of those sources. The spouse who claims something is nonmarital has to prove it. That proof is paper: statements, deeds, closing documents, going back as far as the claim does.

Growth is where it gets contested. A premarital account that grew on its own is one thing. A premarital business that grew because one or both of you worked in it for fifteen years is another.

How the court divides it

The court makes a just and equitable division of the marital property, without regard to marital misconduct (§ 518.58). It considers the length of the marriage, each spouse’s age, health, income, and employability, and what each contributed, including as a homemaker. The law conclusively presumes that each spouse made a substantial contribution while you lived together.

In practice most divisions come out near equal. Three rules move the numbers:

  • The valuation date. Assets are valued as of the first scheduled prehearing settlement conference, unless you agree on another date or the court finds a different one fair (§ 518.58, subd. 1). In a rising or falling market, the date is worth arguing about.
  • Assets that went missing. If a spouse transfers, hides, or disposes of marital assets during the case or in anticipation of it, outside ordinary business and living expenses, the court puts both of you back where you would have been (§ 518.58, subd. 1a).
  • Hardship. If one spouse would otherwise be left with too little, the court can award up to half of the other’s nonmarital property (§ 518.58, subd. 2).

What is usually on the table

  • The house. One spouse buys the other out, or it is sold. The number comes from an appraisal, and a buyout usually means a refinance.
  • Retirement accounts and pensions. The share earned during the marriage is marital. Dividing it takes a separate court order that the plan will accept, and the order has to be drafted to the plan’s rules.
  • Businesses and farms. These need a valuation, and a plan for paying one spouse out without breaking the operation.
  • Debt. Divided along with the assets. The court’s order binds the two of you. It does not bind the lender.
  • Cryptocurrency. It is property like any other, and it is harder to hide than people think. Every transfer sits on a public ledger, and reading that ledger is work I know.

Questions people ask first

The house is in my name only. Is it mine?

Title does not decide it. If it was bought during the marriage with marital money, it is marital.

What about my inheritance?

Nonmarital, if you can trace it. Money that sat in its own account is easy. Money that went into the joint account and paid for a kitchen is hard.

Is everything split down the middle?

Often close to it, by value. Not item by item. One spouse may keep the house and the other the retirement account, with a payment to even it out.

What if I think my spouse is hiding money?

Both spouses have to disclose what they own and owe, and the disclosures can be tested with subpoenas to the banks and brokerages. Tell me why you think so and what you have seen.
